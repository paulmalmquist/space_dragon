# Space Dragon — Prologue & Act I: Build the Dragon

**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** October 28, 2026  
**Owner:** Paul Malmquist  
**Status:** current narrative baseline with explicitly labeled treatment proposals; documentation only.

> The equipment was sent deliberately. Where it landed—and who found it—was the accident.
>
> We weren't chosen. We were in range. Then we chose to help.

## 1. Authority and story foundation

Read this document before implementing the opening. It supersedes conflicting origin-story and opening-sequence assumptions in the [initial specification](../space-dragon-spec-v0.1.md) and earlier brainstorming. The initial specification remains a historical design baseline; unrelated technical requirements are not replaced here.

### Confirmed direction from the owner

- The prologue/Act I is about **building the dragon**. The complete dragon is not available at the start.
- The opening takes place with the family **working at the living-room computer**.
- Something crashes in the backyard. It accelerates construction and development and supplies the family's specialized suits.
- Other objects arrive separately and scatter around familiar places in town. The family retrieves them.
- When the components are combined, they construct the mecha dragon according to a pre-existing design. The dragon is **not something the family imagined or invented**.
- There is a reason the equipment was sent: an **SOS from another galaxy**, connected to a mission that will take the family to **a different universe**.
- Earth was the closest planet within range of the SOS. The equipment reached the family accidentally; the senders did not select this family in advance.
- Paul, Maria, Luke, Luna, and Lyla remain the five-person crew, with their established suit specialties.
- Familiar-town retrieval adventures combine early learning, exploration, fun racing, and playful combat. The supportive neighborhood and the fictional across-the-street antagonist remain part of the story.

### Treatment conventions

The scene order below develops that foundation. Exact dialogue, module names, the module-to-location assignments, suit adaptation, and the technical explanation for crossing universes are **PROPOSED**, not additional confirmed lore. Unresolved story questions are listed at the end.

### Continuity rule: galaxy versus universe

Preserve both elements of the owner's premise; do not silently replace the destination with another star system in our universe. A proposed explanation is that a temporary opening connects universes, making Earth the nearest reachable planet on our side of the signal. Its final mechanism and the sender's exact location remain open.

The dragon's established light-speed travel and its ability to cross between universes are distinct story capabilities. Do not claim that ordinary light-speed travel alone explains the crossing.

## 2. Act-level synopsis

An ordinary evening at the living-room computer is interrupted by a damaged transmission and a crash in the backyard. The recovered object equips the family with specialized suits and reveals other signals around town. Its incomplete diagrams show a mechanical dragon, but it cannot finish assembly or explain the full mission without the scattered components.

The family visits places they know, helps people, learns to use the suits, retrieves the components, and frustrates a neighbor's increasingly elaborate interference. Each recovery restores part of the construction system and another fragment of the SOS.

With every component reunited, the equipment constructs the dragon. The restored transmission reveals that it is a rescue vehicle intended to take its crew to the people who called for help, in another universe. The family understands the mission before deciding to depart. The choice to answer the call closes Act I; answering it begins Act II.

## 3. Prologue — The Signal

### Scene A1-00: At the living-room computer

**Purpose:** begin in a recognizable family setting, not with a prophecy or an exposition-heavy alien history.

Paul and Luke are working at the computer. Maria, Luna, and Lyla are nearby and join the moment. Their ordinary computer activity can be chosen later. It must not establish that they designed the dragon or already built the suits.

The speakers make an unfamiliar sound. A broken image appears on-screen: an indistinct voice, an unknown place, and a recurring symbol. The family did not deliberately summon it.

**Draft dialogue:**

> Luke: “What's that?”
>
> Paul: “I don't know. I didn't open it.”

The picture breaks up. A bright streak passes the window, followed by an impact in the backyard.

### Scene A1-01: Something in the backyard

The family approaches together. The object is a compact assembly and communication unit, provisionally called the **Builder Core**. It carries the same symbol as the transmission.

The Core unfolds and projects an incomplete mechanical-dragon schematic. This is its own stored design—not a scan of a drawing made by the family.

The damaged display indicates that other components are missing. More streaks separate overhead and disappear toward town.

**Draft dialogue:**

> Maria: “Everybody stay together.”
>
> Luke: “Is that a dragon?”

**Proposed presentation:** spectacular light and a shallow landing mark, without injured people, destroyed houses, or a realistic disaster. The pods may brake before impact; that detail is not yet fixed.

## 4. Act I — Retrieval and discovery

### Scene A1-02: Five suits

The Core supplies the suits before the first town expedition. The family does not already own them, and children do not have to earn their defining powers through tests.

**Proposed explanation:** rescue equipment adapts to the people who activate it. This preserves recognizable, individualized suits without implying that the senders knew the family beforehand. Why it can equip exactly five people remains a lore decision, not evidence of destiny.

| Family member | Established suit identity | Proposed introductory contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paul | Iron Man-inspired powered armor | Move a heavy panel and repair a damaged connection. |
| Luke | Iron Spider-inspired suit | Reach a high fitting with mechanical limbs and a tether. |
| Maria | Purple healer suit | Protect the group during a power test and restore suit energy. |
| Luna | Pink suit; slippery goo | Free a stuck mechanism and later make a charging enemy slip. |
| Lyla | Information gathering and storage; suit color open | Scan the Core, record signal fragments, and organize discoveries. |

Each suit gets a brief reveal. Routine family actions can be automatic or cinematic; the player need not complete five long tutorials. Retractable helmets preserve recognizable faces. Detailed power limits and upgrade trees remain design proposals.

### Scene A1-03: A different kind of town map

The Core identifies nearby component signals. The map contains familiar destinations, not unexplained interstellar locations. The family recognizes places they normally visit.

**Draft dialogue:**

> Luke: “There's one by my school!”
>
> Maria: “And that one's somewhere we already go.”
>
> Paul: “Looks like we've got a different kind of shopping list.”

The parts were not deliberately addressed to particular schools or shops. Their arrival locations are accidental. Their signals, visible landing traces, and helpful witnesses supply understandable clues.

The living-room computer becomes a planning station connected to the Core. The Core remains at home; the suits provide a practical way to find and bring back the other components.

### Scene A1-04: Recover the scattered components

**Mission pattern:** choose a signal, visit a familiar place, investigate, complete a short adventure, retrieve the component, return home, and learn something new.

The following is a **proposed six-component treatment**, not a locked level count, traversal order, or production scope. The first playable can use fewer missions. School and workplace environments must be fictionalized; this public document intentionally omits school names, precise layouts, and restricted workplace details.

| Familiar location | Proposed component | Proposed adventure | Story contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The girls' school, fictionalized | Assembly Module | Find connectors, match shapes, and follow an iguana carrying the last piece. | The recovered objects belong to one construction system. |
| Luke's elementary school, fictionalized | Navigation Module | Follow a signal through picture or letter checkpoints, then complete an obstacle-course chase. | The dragon has a destination; it is not just a vehicle for sightseeing. |
| Costco-inspired store setting | Power Module | Move a heavy pod, fit energy cells into sockets, and bring it home in a cargo-cart race. | Enough power returns to replay another damaged message fragment. |
| Trader Joe's-inspired store setting | Life-Support Module | Restore scrambled controls through sorting and matching, then recover a runaway delivery cart. | The equipment is intended to carry living passengers. |
| Target-inspired store setting | Wing-Control Module | Track a signal, recover controls stolen by robot iguanas, and evade mosquito drones. | The flight systems respond together; a launch is becoming possible. |
| Fictional rocket-company facility inspired by Paul's work | Propulsion Module | Complete a small test sequence and vehicle challenge, then overcome an armored-alligator ambush. | The last component enables final assembly and restoration of the complete SOS. |

Locations should feel familiar without reproducing actual school access routes, identifying other children, or depicting restricted base operations. No workplace recording or external upload permission is implied. Fictional names can replace real store names in the finished game.

Optional discoveries can add decorations, sounds, or archive entries. Required story components must remain distinguishable from optional collectibles.

### Scene A1-05: Returning home and uncovering the SOS

Every return provides a visible payoff. The family docks the component around the Core. A corresponding section of the dragon schematic lights up, and a safe demonstration previews the system: wing movement, cockpit instruments, an engine sound, or simulated flight.

**Construction continuity:** the complete physical dragon is assembled only after all required components are reunited. Progress can be shown through the hologram, docking stations, and test mechanisms; do not casually give the player a finished flyable dragon halfway through the retrieval arc.

The proposed revelation sequence is:

1. Something arrived, and its components are scattered.
2. The pieces belong to one machine; someone sent them deliberately.
3. The transmission is a request for help, not an instruction to conquer or a prophecy about this family.
4. The dragon is the means to reach the source of the request.
5. The restored message reveals that the journey leads into another universe.

Lyla preserves the fragments in the Family Archive. She can replay and compare discoveries, but she cannot reveal information the family has not recovered. The exact wording of the SOS and the identity of its sender remain open.

### Scene A1-06: Trouble across the street

The neighborhood encourages the project and helps with retrievals, repairs, and celebrations. The fictional across-the-street neighbor objects to the strange equipment and the prospect of a giant mecha dragon occupying the cul-de-sac.

Her established visual brief is a blonde woman in her late fifties. Use a fictional name and design, not an identifiable portrait or allegation about a real person.

**Proposed escalation:** complaints become misleading signs, stolen components, iguana interference, and eventually an armored-alligator or drone encounter. The final confrontation threatens recovery of a component, not the children's ability to finish a reading question.

**Draft dialogue during a full-size holographic preview:**

> Neighbor: “Please tell me that is a decoration.”
>
> Paul: “It's a construction preview.”
>
> Neighbor: “A preview of what?”
>
> The holographic dragon spreads its wings.

She is the immediate local antagonist. Do not assume that she caused the inter-universe emergency. The source of her advanced henchmen is still open; a fallen fragment that upgrades her equipment is only a possible explanation.

## 5. Act I finale — The dragon and the decision

### Scene A1-07: Assembly complete

The last component locks into its docking point. For the first time, the whole system answers as one.

**Proposed staging:** the five suits assist with a final calibration. The Core displays the complete stored design. Mechanical sections unfold, connect, and rise into place; the spine forms, legs settle, and wings open above the cul-de-sac. Five seats illuminate inside.

The family has not invented or redesigned the machine. Their accomplishment is recovering the components, making them work together, and bringing the rescue vehicle into being.

The neighborhood celebrates. The neighbor realizes that the dragon really can fly.

### Scene A1-08: The full message

The restored communications system plays the complete SOS. It now establishes that the equipment was sent to enable whoever received it to reach the people asking for help. Earth was within reach; this family happened to find the equipment.

The family learns the nature of the journey before leaving. The equipment must not secretly abduct them or conceal an irreversible departure behind a child-facing “play” button.

**Draft dialogue:**

> Luke: “They sent all this because they need somebody?”
>
> Paul: “Yes.”
>
> Luke: “And it came to us?”
>
> Maria: “It did.”

They decide together to answer. Their significance comes from what they choose to do, not from a hidden bloodline, preordained identity, or a design that was secretly theirs all along.

**Act I ending:** the dragon is complete, the purpose of the SOS is understood sufficiently to act, and the family commits to the journey. A first launch or view of the crossing can serve as the closing image; the precise cut between acts is open.

**Act II begins:** travel to the source of the SOS and discover who called, what happened, and what help is needed. Do not add an unrelated sightseeing campaign between assembly and answering the signal.

## 6. Learning and action within the story

These are proposed game-design rules, not claims that a curriculum or assessment has already been implemented or validated.

- Learning difficulty follows the child playing, not the selected family character. Luna and Lyla are three; Luke is five. Treat these as starting design contexts, not proof of any child's skill level.
- Early activities can use spoken instructions, familiar-letter recognition, matching, small quantities, shapes, and simple patterns. More advanced variants can introduce taught letter sounds, appropriately supported word reading, counting combinations, and longer directions.
- Distinguish matching a visible letter from recognizing its name or knowing its sound. Do not record one as proof of the others.
- Teach, demonstrate, and provide hints. Assisted completion still advances the story and awards the component. No suit, required part, or family departure is withheld because a child needs reading help.
- Keep racing and combat fun in their own right. Do not ask a beginning reader to decode text while steering at speed. Pause the action or use a safe station for learning interactions.
- Use adjustable steering assistance, clear warnings, and forgiving retries. Playful combat resolves in slipping, capture, disarming, disabling machines, or retreat—not gore or frightening family injury.
- The archive can replay discoveries and a parent-read mission recap. Avoid sibling rankings or unsupported mastery claims.

## 7. Implementation boundary

This document changes the story, not the implementation status. No game code, environments, photo imports, character models, tests, or deployments are delivered by this narrative commit.

The original specification's “Launch Day” flight-first loop is no longer the chronological opening of the story. It may remain useful as a separately labeled flight prototype. Its old scope is not approval to skip Act I in the eventual narrative release.

A proposed story-first prototype is: living-room opening, backyard encounter, brief suit reveals, one town recovery mission, a persistent recovered-component state, and a return-home revelation. That is a production option, not authorization to build it now.

The future **Line Leader** system remains bookmarked: directly control one family member while the active party follows automatically, with smaller groups later. Writing town scenes does not automatically move full walking, navigation, split parties, or multiplayer into the first implementation. Guided scenes and compact challenges can establish the story first.

Preserve the planned `/space-dragon` route and existing homepage. Keep the [Blender character handoff](../BLENDER_CHARACTER_HANDOFF.md) separate from narrative approval; no asset transfer or model-generation success is implied here.

## 8. Open decisions and continuity checks

### Open decisions

- Who sent the SOS, what threatened them, and what help they expect from unknown responders.
- The precise relationship between the sender's galaxy and the destination universe; crossing mechanics, range, and return travel.
- Why the equipment scattered, how the suits adapt, and why there are five available suits.
- The Core's final name and personality, the dragon's visual design and personality, and Lyla's suit color.
- Final number and order of retrieval missions, component-to-location assignments, and fictional location names.
- The neighbor's fictional identity, the source of her technology, and her role beyond Act I.
- The production milestone that should be built first and the exact transition into Act II.

### Review before implementing or revising

- Does the opening remain at the living-room computer without making the family the dragon's inventors?
- Does the first arrival supply the suits, with other components recovered around town?
- Are deliberate dispatch, accidental arrival, and the family's choice clearly distinct?
- Does the full dragon wait until the required components are reunited?
- Are the learning challenges supported rather than punitive, with real racing and combat also present?
- Does the completed message explain the destination before the family chooses to leave?
- Are proposed lore details still identified as proposals, with the major SOS questions left open?
- Is the next adventure answering the SOS in another universe, rather than silently reverting to the old sightseeing origin?
